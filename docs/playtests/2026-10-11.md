Fourth monitored test play and the first on spec 007 (pending orders) and
spec 008 (markets that bite), build `bb3ae2a`. It is also the first played
on Windows, from the share drive, using the CI playtest package. Default
balance, one AI rival (Northfield Minerals). One game, seed
362503725352500, played to day 1275 (Y4 M7) in about 24 minutes. Trace
`playtest/trace-20261010-223618.jsonl`, 1,651 records. The trace was
recorded on Windows and replayed on Linux: every one of the 1,275 days
matched the client's state hash. No screenshots or markers were taken.

The owner's own impressions are not yet recorded here; what follows is read
from the trace.

## Findings, by weight

### 1. The player never got clear of break-even
From day 540 to the end, 735 days, both ores the player could dig sat near
or below the cost of digging them. Copper break-even for this mine was
about $460 to $520 a tonne and copper traded at $405 to $860; iron
break-even was about $46 and iron traded at $33 to $68. The company ended
with a quarter of its starting cash and everything idle. The price rule is
working as specified: supply is base demand plus company sales, and with
iron sales of only 774 t a day against 4,000 t of base demand the price
was still 0.53 of base, because the demand walk and the construction slump
had taken demand down by about a fifth and elasticity is 1.5. A player
with one or two mines has no way to move that. Whether this is "markets
that bite" or simply a game the player cannot win is the owner's call; the
sweeps did not show it because both companies there are AIs that keep
building.

**The owner's impression (after play):** "The squeeze felt like my actions
were flooding the market alone, so there was no way to operate any mine
profitably, even if I added mines, I would just have caused more flooding
and more loss of profitability."

**The numbers agree.** With the shipped data, one mine at base output is a
large share of the whole world's demand, and the price falls with the 1.5
power of the supply ratio (2.0 for lithium):

| Ore | One mine, share of base demand | Price after 1, 2, 3, 5 mines (x base, demand steady) | Mines in the world to reach break-even |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Iron | 10% | 0.87, 0.76, 0.67, 0.54 | 6 |
| Copper | 11% | 0.85, 0.74, 0.65, 0.52 | 6 |
| Nickel | 22% | 0.74, 0.58, 0.46, 0.33 | 2 |
| Lithium | 25% | 0.64, 0.44, 0.33, 0.20 | 2 |

So the player's first mine alone takes about 14% off its own price, a
second mine in the same ore takes another 11 points off both, and six
mines anywhere in the world put iron or copper at break-even before any
dip in demand. Building more cannot be the answer to low prices, which
leaves the player nothing to do but idle. Spec 008 measured how often
price sat below break-even, and passed; it did not measure how much of
that one mine causes. A manual sale has no size limit either: the 4,872 t
sold on day 751 was nine days of world copper demand, and it feeds the
30-day supply average, so selling a held pile floods the market too
(copper went from $585 to $548 over the following month).

Spec 009 as written would make this worse: a large mine doubles output.

### 2. The losing-mine notice repeats on a mine that hovers
Plot #77 raised the notice on days 544, 586, 664, 735, 875 and 920, each
time stopping the clock, for losses of $660 to $2,100 a day against a
running cost of $27,000. One profitable day between runs re-arms it. A
mine sitting on break-even produces a stream of stops that say nothing
new. It needs a threshold (a loss worth mentioning) or a longer memory.

### 3. Cash went negative with credit unused
The state records show cash of $-783k on days 840 and 870 with zero
insolvent months, and forced sales of stock on days 811, 841 and 871. The
player had $10M of loan capacity and never used it. Open questions: is
negative cash between month ends intended, and does the interface make
borrowing an obvious answer to a squeeze? The Earth strategy's system 4
(failure in visible steps) covers the general case.

### 4. Holding stock costs more than it earns
Storage fees over the game came to about $1.45M, charged in twelve
months. The one deliberate hold (days 664 to 751) raised the copper sold
from about $470 to $600 a tonne on 4,872 t, roughly $630k, against $430k
of storage in those three months. The margin for the decision is thin and
the player cannot see it in advance.

### 5. Lithium sat at two to four times base and nobody could sell it
Lithium ranged from $3,900 to $60,800 against a base of $14,000 with zero
supply all game. The player's first three research projects were survey
techniques. Nothing told the player that the richest market was out of
reach or what it would take to reach it.

### 6. Both opening bids at the asking price lost
The player bid the ask on #29 and #31 and lost both on day 46; the one bid
above the ask won. The opening left the player with the dearest of the
three plots.

### 7. Smaller things
- The mothball notice reads "The mine on plot #11 changed operating
  status." It should say what changed.
- The Market tab abbreviates prices while the mine panel shows an exact
  break-even (already recorded with spec 008).
- No F9 markers were taken, so there is no screenshot evidence of the
  interface on Windows.

## Spec 008's prediction

"Non-routine player orders per game year after year 3 rise to 8 or more."
Not decidable: the game ended half a year into the window. In those 195
days the player gave 6 orders (4 sell-policy changes, 2 mothballs), all of
them responses to price, which would be about 11 a year if it held. The
second half of the prediction, a mine idled because of price, is met
twice.
